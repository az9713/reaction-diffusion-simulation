Goal

Produce a single contact-sheet image that shows every stationary and moving pattern this solver can make. Then find the moving localised structures (gliders, u-skate) that a random seed never reaches.

Constraint: change one thing at a time. The solver in demo.py stays as it is.

Part 1 — The rulebook: three parameters

The Gray-Scott system has four constants: D_u, D_v, f, k. Only three of them change the pattern.

Rescale space, and one diffusion constant disappears. Divide both diffusion constants by D_u, and the equations keep the same shape. Only the ratio D_v/D_u survives. The absolute size of D changes the zoom only — larger blobs, identical shapes.

So the rulebook has three axes: f, k, and D_v/D_u.

AxisCurrent valueWhat it changesSweep it?
f (feed) 0.0545 / 0.0367 Pattern class. Main axis. Yes — stage 1
k (kill) 0.062 / 0.0649 Pattern class. Main axis. Yes — stage 1
D_v/D_u 0.5 Deforms the f-k map. Changes feature size. Optional — stage 3
D_u (absolute) 1.0 Zoom only. No new patterns. No

Two hard limits on the diffusion axis

  • Stability ceiling. The 9-point Laplacian in demo.py:37-42 has a most negative eigenvalue of -1.6. Explicit Euler is stable only for D·dt < 1.25. The current D_u = 1.0 with dt = 1 is close to that ceiling. You cannot raise D_u much. Verified numerically.
  • Ordering. D_v must stay below D_u. The activator v must diffuse slower than the inhibitor u, or the Turing instability never starts.

Good news: published f and k values transfer directly.

This file uses exactly Karl Sims' scheme — same Laplacian weights (0.2 orthogonal, 0.05 diagonal), D_u=1.0, D_v=0.5, dt=1. Both existing pairs are his published values: coral 0.0545 / 0.062 and mitosis 0.0367 / 0.0649.

Against the other common codes that use D_u=0.16, D_v=0.08, the only difference is a spatial zoom of sqrt(1.0/0.16) = 2.5×. The reaction terms are untouched, so the f and k coordinates mean the same thing. Feature size is 2.5× larger here. Plan tile sizes for that.

Part 2 — The seed is not a fourth axis

The three parameters set the rulebook. They decide which stable patterns can exist at all. The seed does not change the rulebook. The seed chooses which of those patterns you fall into — which basin of attraction you start inside.

So varying the seed adds no new region to the map. It reveals extra solutions inside regions the map already contains.

Why you cannot sweep it

The seed is a field, not a number. It holds one value per pixel — tens of thousands of dimensions. You cannot grid-search that. You must design specific starting shapes and test them one at a time.

Where gliders and u-skate live

They need a bistable region. There two states are stable at once: the blank state (u=1, v=0) and a small travelling blob. A seed in such a region either dies or survives as a compact moving object.

Random noise almost never lands in the surviving basin. Noise fills the grid instead. That is why the existing demos show mazes and dividing spots, and no gliders.

Unverified. Reported u-skate coordinates are near f ≈ 0.062, k ≈ 0.0609 for ratio-2 codes. I have not run this here. Treat it as a starting point to check in stage 2, not as a fact.

No moving localised structure appeared at or near those coordinates. The flag stands, now with evidence behind it.

The one legitimate seed knob

You can build a 1-parameter family by hand: two blobs, separated by a gap. That single distance decides whether the pair annihilates, sits still, or skates away. It is a designed axis, not an intrinsic one — you choose it, the equations do not hand it to you.

Seeding already changes results in this repo. The comment at demo.py:25 records it: isolated spots are sub-critical in the mitosis regime and dissolve, so that demo needs at least 36 seeds on a 480×270 grid. Same parameters, different seed, different outcome. That is the rulebook-versus-basin split in one line of existing code.

Stage 1 — The f-k contact sheet

Deliverable: one PNG. A 12 × 12 grid of tiles. Each tile is one simulation at one (f, k) pair, labelled with its values and a static-or-dynamic marker.

Settings

  • Fix D_u = 1.0, D_v = 0.5, dt = 1.
  • Sweep f from 0.010 to 0.090. Sweep k from 0.045 to 0.070.
  • Tile size 192 px square for the first run. Do not use 128 px — features are 2.5× larger here, and a 128 px tile shows two spots and tells you nothing. Go to 224 or 256 px only if 192 px looks cramped; the run time rises steeply (20 → 50 → 65 min).
  • Use one identical seed in every tile: a central noisy square, about 20 px.
  • Run about 8000 steps. Record the last 50 frames for the motion metric.

Performance — measured, not estimated

Stack all tiles into one (144, H, W) array and step them together in one vectorised loop. The Laplacian and the reaction step both broadcast over the leading axis without change.

Then replace the eight np.roll calls with one scipy.ndimage.convolve(Z, K, mode="wrap"). scipy is already installed. I checked the result against demo.laplacian: maximum absolute difference 2.2e-16 — identical to floating-point noise. The speed gain is 5×.

Method144 tiles, 192 px8000 steps
np.roll, float641891 ms/step (at 224 px)252 min
np.roll, float32774 ms/step103 min
scipy.ndimage.convolve, float32149 ms/step20 min

Measured on this machine, 12 cores. Use float32, not float64. It halves the memory traffic and the pattern classes do not need the extra digits.

Correction to my earlier estimate. I said batched stepping would take seconds. That was wrong by three orders of magnitude. Stage 1 is a 20-minute job at 192 px, not a 5-second one. Plan the wall clock accordingly — see the cost table below.

Static against dynamic

A final frame cannot tell a frozen maze from a travelling wave. Use a number instead: mean |Δv| per pixel over the final 50 steps, after the pattern settles.

  • Near zero — static. Stripes, spots, mazes.
  • High — dynamic. Waves, solitons, dividing spots, chaos.
  • Exactly zero with v.sum() near zero — dead tile.

Draw the value as a small coloured bar on each tile.

Acceptance check — calibration

The two existing demos are free ground truth. Include their exact coordinates in the grid, or run them as two extra tiles.

PASS only if:

  • tile at f=0.0545, k=0.062 shows a branching maze;
  • tile at f=0.0367, k=0.0649 shows dividing spots;
  • both are marked dynamic-or-static consistently with the existing MP4s.

If those two tiles are wrong, the sweep harness is wrong. Fix the harness before reading any other tile.

Also record

Mark tiles where v.sum() settles low and non-zero and the pattern does not fill the domain. Those are the bistable candidates. Stage 2 uses only those.

Stage 2 — Designed seeds inside the bistable window

Deliverable: a short strip of runs, plus a printed verdict per run. Not a sweep — a handful of experiments.

Scope

Use only the (f, k) pairs that stage 1 marked bistable. Add the reported u-skate pair f ≈ 0.062, k ≈ 0.0609 as a candidate to check.

The four seeds

  1. Single blob. One circle of v=1, u=0.5. Baseline: does it survive, grow, or die?
  2. Blob pair. Two circles, separated by a gap g. Sweep g over about 8 values. This is the one real 1D knob.
  3. Asymmetric blob. One circle with a bite removed on one side. Broken symmetry is what gives a structure a direction to travel in.
  4. Gradient blob. One circle with a left-to-right ramp in v.

Use a fixed RNG seed. Add no noise, or add a tiny fixed amount. Noise destroys the very symmetry you are controlling.

The glider detector — nearly free

run_sim already tracks v.sum() and the x-centroid at demo.py:90-92. Add the y-centroid, and the detector is two more lines.

  • Glider: v.sum() flat, centroid drifting at near-constant speed.
  • Static soliton: v.sum() flat, centroid still.
  • Growth: v.sum() rising — it filled the domain, not a localised structure.
  • Death: v.sum() falling to zero.

Warning: the grid wraps (np.roll). A glider crossing the edge makes the centroid jump. Unwrap the centroid track before you measure the speed, or stop the run before the first crossing.

Acceptance check

PASS if at least one (f, k, seed) combination gives flat v.sum() and a centroid speed above zero, held for 2000 steps.

If nothing passes, report that plainly. A null result here is a real result — it means the bistable window in this parameterisation is narrower than the stage 1 grid resolution. The next step would be a finer local grid, not more seeds.

Stage 3 — Diffusion ratio (optional)

Deliverable: two more contact sheets, identical to stage 1, at D_v/D_u = 0.3 and 0.7.

Three sheets, not a 4D cube. Expect the map to deform and the feature size to change. Do not expect new pattern classes.

Keep D_u = 1.0 in both. Keep the ratio below 1.

Run this only if stage 1 leaves an interesting question. It is a nice-to-have.

Files and interface

One new file: sweep.py. Stages as flags. No new module, no config file, no class hierarchy.

python sweep.py --stage1                 # f-k contact sheet -> sweep_fk.png
python sweep.py --stage1 --ratio 0.3     # stage 3, same code path
python sweep.py --stage2                 # designed seeds, prints verdicts
python sweep.py --selftest                # calibration check only
  • Do not modify demo.py. sweep.py carries its own batched float32 scipy stepper, with D_u and D_v as arguments. Import laplacian and step from demo.py only as the reference the selftest checks against. Two steppers is correct here: the demo one is tuned for one grid and readability, the sweep one for 144 grids and speed.
  • Write PNGs through the existing ffmpeg call pattern, or through a plain numpy tile-assembly plus one ffmpeg write. Add no image library.
  • Add the sweep outputs to .gitignore unless you want them in the repo.

Order of work

  1. Write the batched scipy stepper in sweep.py. Leave demo.py alone.
  2. The load-bearing check. Run one tile of the batched stepper against demo.step with the coral parameters, for 200 steps. The two must agree to floating-point tolerance. I already confirmed the Laplacians match to 2.2e-16; this check extends that to the full reaction step and to float32.
  3. Run stage 1. Check the two calibration tiles first. Stop if they fail.
  4. Read the sheet. Record which tiles are bistable.
  5. Run stage 2 on those tiles only.
  6. Run stage 3 if a question remains.

The largest single lever is not the model. It is the calibration check in stage 1. Catching a broken harness on two known tiles costs one turn. Discovering it after you have read the whole sheet costs the whole sheet.

Known risks

  • A single seeding hides exotica. Stage 1 uses one noise seed, so it will not show seeding-sensitive structures. That is by design — stage 2 exists for them. Do not read "no gliders on the sheet" as "no gliders".
  • Settling time varies by region. Low-f tiles evolve slowly. 8000 steps may not settle them. If the motion metric looks high everywhere in one corner, raise the step count there before you call it dynamic.
  • The u-skate coordinates are unverified in this code. Marked above. Confirm or reject in stage 2 and record the result. Outcome: rejected — swept finely and not reproduced.
  • Clipping. step clips u and v to [0,1] at demo.py:67-68. Near the stability ceiling that clip can hide a divergence instead of showing it. Assert on the pre-clip range in the sweep, so an unstable tile reports itself instead of looking like a pattern.

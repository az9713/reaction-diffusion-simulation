The unified mathematics of Gray-Scott patterns

Coral, mitosis, solitons, worms, and the u-skate glider — five behaviours of one two-line equation, and the geometry in parameter space that separates them.

Every number in this document is either derived here or measured by the solver in this repository. Where a derivation predicts a number, the measured value is printed beside it.

0. Five pictures, one equation

Put two chemicals in a thin tray. Chemical u is fed in steadily from above. Chemical v eats u and turns it into more of itself, and it is drained away at a steady rate. Both spread sideways by ordinary diffusion, and u spreads twice as fast as v. That is the whole system. It has two adjustable knobs: the feed rate f and the extra kill rate k.

From that one setup, and nothing else, the tray can produce a branching solid that grows like stony coral; a field of spots that swell and split in two like dividing cells; single frozen dots that sit still forever; growing snakes that fill the tray; and a small structure that glides across the tray at constant speed, keeping its shape, forever. The last one is called the u-skate glider.

The obvious guess is that the two knobs pick the pattern — that (f, k) is an address and each address holds one picture. That guess is wrong, and this document is about why. The knobs choose which steady states exist and which of them are stable. When more than one is stable, the starting condition chooses which one the tray actually finds. Two experiments at identical (f, k) can produce different pictures, permanently.

This repository ran 320 simulation tiles across five parameter sweeps looking for the glider, including two tiles sitting inside the published parameter band for it, and found none. Nothing was wrong with the parameters. Every seed started from the wrong state. The mathematics below says exactly why that could never have worked, and the last sections turn the same mathematics into the recipe that works on the first attempt.

The route:

  • Historical background says where the equation came from, and why it has a square in it. It can be skipped without loss.
  • §1 derives the equations from the reaction, and fixes every symbol.
  • §2 switches diffusion off and finds the steady states. A fold curve appears.
  • §3 asks which of those states survive a nudge. A second curve appears, and the two curves cut the knob plane into three regions.
  • §4 switches diffusion back on and proves that no pattern can grow spontaneously from the empty tray.
  • §5 is the point of the document: with two stable states, the seed selects the pattern.
  • §6 places all five named patterns on the map of §3.
  • §7 proves the scale invariance that makes one solution into a whole family — and that faked a false result in this repository until it was understood.
  • §8 descends to the finite grid, where the stability limit lives.
  • §9 assembles the glider and checks it against measurement.
  • §10 states what this model does not capture.

Historical background: where this equation came from

The system of §1 is usually met as a pattern generator, which makes its form look arbitrary. Why a square on v? Why two knobs and not three? The answers are historical. The system was not built to make patterns, and for its first ten years it had no spatial dimension at all. It was built to settle an argument in chemical reactor engineering. The one design decision that produces every picture in this document was made for a reason that has nothing to do with space.

This section is background. Nothing later depends on it.

A stirred tank, not a tray

P. Gray and S. K. Scott studied the continuously stirred tank reactor (CSTR): a vessel with an impeller, fed continuously with fresh reagent and drained continuously of its contents. The stirring holds the contents uniform, so the vessel has no interior geometry. Its model is a pair of ordinary differential equations — exactly the well-stirred limit that §2 reaches by deleting the Laplacians. Two papers set out the scheme used here.

  • P. Gray and S. K. Scott, Autocatalytic reactions in the isothermal, continuous stirred tank reactor: isolas and other forms of multistability, Chemical Engineering Science 38 (1983) 29–43.
  • P. Gray and S. K. Scott, Autocatalytic reactions in the isothermal CSTR: oscillations and instabilities in the system A + 2B → 3B; B → C, Chemical Engineering Science 39 (1984) 1087–1097.

The second title is reaction (1.1) written as chemistry, with A for u, B for v and C for the inert product. The tank supplies both knobs, and neither is a free dial invented for convenience. The feed rate f is the pump: it is the reciprocal of the mean time a molecule spends in the vessel, so 1/f is a residence time in seconds. The kill rate k is the rate constant of the decay v → P. Both are quantities a reactor operator sets or measures.

The motivation: taking heat out of the explanation

Read the vocabulary in those two titles. Multistability, and in the body of the work ignition, extinction and hysteresis. The words are borrowed from combustion, and that is the point of the exercise.

Reactor engineers already knew that a tank can jump between two very different steady states at one setting, can ignite, can quench, and can oscillate. The standard explanation was thermal feedback: the reaction releases heat, the heat raises the temperature, the higher temperature accelerates the reaction. That is a positive loop through the energy balance, and it is enough to produce all of the listed behaviour.

Gray and Scott asked whether the heat was necessary. Their answer was no. Cubic autocatalysis alone reproduces the whole list at constant temperature. That is why the word isothermal appears in both titles. Chemical self-amplification — v making more v — replaces thermal self-amplification, and the energy balance can be deleted entirely.

So the system was designed from the start as the smallest scheme that still produces multiple stable states. That is worth holding on to, because it is exactly what §3 finds and what §5 turns into the central result of this document. The multistability is not a curiosity discovered later in a pattern model. It is the property the model was built to have.

Why the square on v

This is the design decision. Consider the alternative, quadratic autocatalysis:

u + v → 2v, with rate uv.

One molecule of each is enough, so the growth rate is proportional to v itself. Removal is also proportional to v. Halving v halves both sides of the competition at once, so the outcome does not depend on how much v is present. There is no threshold, and there is nothing for a seed to decide.

Now the cubic reaction (1.1). It needs two molecules of v to meet one of u at the same place. Two-body meetings are rare when v is rare, so by mass action the growth rate carries a square while removal stays linear. Deleting diffusion and factoring the v equation (1.3) shows what that buys:

dv/dt = uv2 − (f + k)v = v (uv − (f + k)).

Chemical v grows where uv > f + k and dies where the inequality reverses. The live states of §2 are the equality case. The square is therefore the minimum nonlinearity that creates a threshold at all, and the threshold is an amplitude, not a parameter setting.

Three consequences follow immediately, and each one is proved properly later.

  • Every term in the v equation carries a factor of v. So v = 0 solves it for every f and k. The dead state is structural, not accidental — see §2.
  • A small v is below threshold, so it shrinks. Nothing grows out of noise — the content of §4.
  • The seed must therefore clear the threshold by itself. That is §5, and it is why this repository's 320 tiles found nothing.
The threshold, in the glider's own numbers. The published glider runs at f + k = 0.062 + 0.06093 = 0.12293. Its seed sets a background of u = 0.5, v = 0.3 (§9), giving uv = 0.15. That clears the threshold by 22 percent, so the background sustains v. A dead background has uv = 0 and cannot. The recipe is sized against this inequality. This is the diffusion-free estimate; diffusion moves v out of a region and u into it, so it shifts the true boundary. The exact statement is §5.

Pearson put the tank into space

Gray and Scott produced no patterns, because a stirred tank cannot hold one. The two Laplacians were added by John E. Pearson, in Complex Patterns in a Simple System, Science 261 (5118), 189–192, 1993. Pearson integrated the system in two dimensions, gave u the larger diffusion coefficient, and swept (f, k) to produce a published map of regimes. The names coral and mitosis used throughout this document descend from that map. Pearson's own abstract gives the ancestry: the scheme is a variant of Selkov's model of glycolysis, and is due to Gray and Scott.

1. The model

The Gray-Scott system describes an isothermal, continuously stirred tank reactor spread out in space. Three processes act on two dissolved chemicals.

The reaction. One molecule of u meets two of v and becomes three of v:

u + 2v → 3v(1.1)

This is autocatalysis: v is both an input and the output, so the more v is present, the faster more of it is made. By the law of mass action the rate of a reaction is proportional to the product of the concentrations of its inputs, each raised to the number of copies consumed. With concentrations written u and v and the rate constant scaled to one, that rate is uv2. Each event destroys one u and creates one net v.

The feed. Fresh solution of pure u at unit concentration is pumped in, and the tank contents are pumped out, both at rate f. This adds f(1 − u) to u: it pushes u towards 1 and does so faster the further away it is. The same outflow removes v at rate fv, since v is not present in the feed.

The kill. Chemical v additionally decays, or is converted to an inert product, at rate k. Combined with the outflow, v is removed at total rate (f + k)v. The sum f + k appears together so often that it is worth naming now; it is the total per-unit removal rate of v.

Diffusion. Both chemicals spread by Fick's law, with diffusion coefficients Du and Dv. The Laplacian ∇2 of a field is the amount by which the field's local average exceeds its value at a point, so diffusion moves each chemical from where it is concentrated towards where it is not.

Adding the four contributions gives the governing equations.

Definition 1 (the Gray-Scott system). For concentration fields u(x,t) and v(x,t) on a domain in the plane,
∂u/∂t = Du∇2u − uv2 + f(1 − u) (1.2)
∂v/∂t = Dv∇2v + uv2 − (f + k)v (1.3)
with f > 0, k > 0, Du > Dv > 0. All four parameters are constants, uniform in space and time.

Three structural features of (1.2)–(1.3) drive everything that follows, and each is worth noticing before any analysis.

  1. The reaction term is shared and opposite. The quantity uv2 is subtracted from u and added to v. The reaction moves material between the two fields and never creates or destroys the total.
  2. Only the feed and the kill break that conservation. The system is open. It is continuously supplied with free energy and continuously drained, which is what allows a structure to persist indefinitely without running down. Every persistent object here is a dissipative structure, held together by throughput, not by a conservation law.
  3. v = 0 is invariant. Both the reaction term and the removal term carry a factor of v. Where v is exactly zero it stays zero, apart from what diffuses in. Chemical v cannot appear from nothing: it can only spread from where it already is. This is the algebraic root of the entire seed-dependence story in §5.

2. The well-stirred limit: what states exist

Start by removing space. If the tray is stirred so thoroughly that both concentrations are uniform, the Laplacians vanish and (1.2)–(1.3) collapse to two ordinary differential equations. A steady state is a pair (u, v) at which both time derivatives are zero, so the mixture sits still forever.

0 = −uv2 + f(1 − u),      0 = uv2 − (f + k)v (2.1)

The second equation factors as v (uv − (f + k)) = 0, which splits the problem into two cases immediately.

Case 1: v = 0. The first equation then reads f(1 − u) = 0, so u = 1. This is the dead state (1, 0): pure feedstock, no reaction, nothing happening. It exists for every f and every k, without condition.

Case 2: v > 0. Then uv = f + k, so u = (f+k)/v. Substituting that into the first equation of (2.1) gives a quadratic in v, and whether it has real roots is the whole question.

Theorem 1 (existence of the live states). The system (2.1) has steady states with v > 0 if and only if
f ≥ 4(f + k)2,   equivalently   k ≤ √f2 − f (2.2)
When the inequality is strict there are exactly two such states, with
v± = f ± √(f2 − 4f(f+k)2)2(f + k),     u± = f + kv± (2.3)
The curve of equality in (2.2) is called the fold curve.
Put u = (f+k)/v into uv2 = f(1 − u). The left side becomes (f+k)v, so
(f + k)v = f − f(f + k)v
Multiply by v (legitimate, since v > 0) and collect:
(f + k)v2 − fv + f(f + k) = 0 (2.4)
This is a quadratic with positive leading coefficient. Its discriminant is f2 − 4f(f+k)2, and real roots exist exactly when that is non-negative. Dividing by f > 0 gives f ≥ 4(f+k)2, the first form of (2.2). Taking square roots of both sides — both are positive — gives √f ≥ 2(f+k), and solving for k gives the second form. The quadratic formula applied to (2.4) yields (2.3); both roots are positive because the coefficients give a positive sum f/(f+k) and a positive product f. ∎

Both live states carry a conventional name. The larger root v+ is the live state: plenty of v, the reaction running hard. The smaller root v− turns out to be a saddle, proved in §3, and it acts as the threshold that a seed must cross. Fig. 2 shows both cases: at the u-skate parameters the two nullclines cross three times, and at the mitosis parameters they cross only once.

The fold condition is not a loose qualitative guide. It is a sharp line, and the four parameter points used in this repository fall on both sides of it by small margins.

3. Which states survive a nudge

Existence is not persistence. A steady state that amplifies every small disturbance will never be observed. The test is linearization: write the state as a steady state plus a small perturbation, keep only terms of first order in the perturbation, and read the growth or decay from the resulting matrix — the Jacobian J, whose entries are the partial derivatives of the two reaction rates with respect to u and v.

J = ( −v2 − f−2uv v22uv − (f+k) ) (3.1)

A two-by-two matrix is stable — both eigenvalues have negative real part — exactly when its trace is negative and its determinant positive. Those two numbers are all we need.

Proposition 1 (the dead state is always stable). At (1, 0) the Jacobian is diagonal with entries −f and −(f+k). Both are negative for every f, k > 0, so the dead state is linearly stable throughout the parameter plane.
Substitute u = 1, v = 0 into (3.1). The off-diagonal entries are −2uv = 0 and v2 = 0, so J is diagonal, and the diagonal entries −v2−f = −f and 2uv−(f+k) = −(f+k) are the eigenvalues. Both are strictly negative because f, k > 0 by Definition 1. ∎

This proposition does more work than its two lines suggest. The dead state is stable everywhere — there is no corner of the knob plane where an empty tray spontaneously comes to life. Everything interesting therefore has to be put in by hand, a point §4 sharpens once diffusion is restored.

The live states are more interesting, and their trace and determinant collapse to remarkably simple expressions.

Proposition 2 (trace and determinant on the live branch). At any steady state with v > 0,
tr J = k − v2,      det J = (f + k)(v2 − f) (3.2)
Hence such a state is linearly stable if and only if v2 > max(f, k).
At any such state uv = f+k, so the single quantity 2uv appearing twice in (3.1) equals 2(f+k). Substituting,
tr J = (−v2 − f) + (2(f+k) − (f+k)) = −v2 − f + f + k = k − v2
For the determinant, expand and use the same substitution:
det J = (−v2−f)(f+k) + 2uv v2 = (f+k) [−v2 − f + 2v2]
which is (f+k)(v2−f). Since f+k > 0, the determinant is positive exactly when v2 > f, and the trace is negative exactly when v2 > k. Stability needs both. ∎

Apply (3.2) to the smaller root. At the fold the two roots coincide at v = √f, and below it v− < √f, so det J < 0 there. A negative determinant means eigenvalues of opposite sign: v− is a saddle, attracting along one direction and repelling along another. It is the threshold between the dead state and the live one, which is exactly the role a seed has to overcome.

The live state is stable only while v+2 > k. The boundary where equality holds is a second curve through the knob plane, and it has a closed form.

Theorem 2 (the Hopf curve, and where it ends). The live state loses stability through a Hopf bifurcation — trace zero with positive determinant — exactly on the curve
(f + k)2 = f √k (3.3)
This curve meets the fold curve of Theorem 1 at the single point f = k = 1/16 = 0.0625, where v = 1/4 and the trace and determinant vanish together.
By Proposition 2 the trace vanishes when v = √k. Substitute that value into the steady-state quadratic (2.4):
(f+k)k − f √k + f(f+k) = 0
The first and third terms share the factor (f+k), giving (f+k)(k+f) = f√k, which is (3.3). For the endpoint, a point on both curves must satisfy the fold equality f = 4(f+k)2 and, since the roots coincide there, v = f/(2(f+k)). The fold equality gives 2(f+k) = √f, so v = f/√f = √f. Trace zero additionally requires v = √k, hence f = k. Putting f = k into the fold equality: f = 4(2f)2 = 16f2, so f = 1/16. Then v = √(1/16) = 1/4, and both (2.2) and (3.3) are satisfied: 4(1/8)2 = 1/16 and (1/8)2 = (1/16)(1/4). ∎

Two curves through one plane make three regions, and Fig. 3 draws them. Region boundaries are exact, computed from (2.2) and (3.3) rather than sketched.

A caution about that meeting point, because it is easy to overstate. The four parameter addresses do not cluster around (1/16, 1/16). What they do is hug the fold curve along its length. Measured as k − (√f/2 − f), three of the four sit within 0.0016 of it, and mitosis is 0.0058 outside — while in the feed direction they are spread widely, with mitosis 41% below 1/16 and u-skate within 1% of it. The fold curve is the organising object; the point f = k = 1/16 is where it terminates together with the Hopf curve, not a place the patterns huddle.

4. Adding diffusion, and the pattern that cannot start

§2 and §3 removed space. Restoring it usually opens a new possibility: Alan Turing's 1952 observation that diffusion can destabilise a state which is stable when stirred, so that a pattern grows on its own out of noise. Most popular accounts of reaction-diffusion patterns stop here and credit Turing. For the dead state of Gray-Scott, that mechanism is simply unavailable, and proving it is what makes the rest of this document necessary.

The standard test perturbs a steady state by a single spatial ripple. Write u = u0 + a exp(iq·x + λt) and similarly for v, where q is the wavevector of the ripple, q = |q| its wavenumber, and λ the growth rate to be found. Since ∇2exp(iq·x) = −q2exp(iq·x), the linearized system becomes an eigenvalue problem for the matrix J − q2D, where D is the diagonal matrix of diffusion coefficients.

Proposition 3 (no Turing instability on the dead state). For every f, k > 0, every Du, Dv > 0 and every wavenumber q, both growth rates at the dead state are strictly negative:
λu(q) = −f − Duq2,     λv(q) = −(f + k) − Dvq2 (4.1)
No spatial pattern can grow spontaneously from the dead state.
By Proposition 1 the Jacobian at (1, 0) is diagonal. Subtracting the diagonal matrix q2D keeps it diagonal, so the eigenvalues are read straight off the diagonal and are exactly (4.1). Both terms in each expression are negative for f, k, Du, Dv > 0 and q2 ≥ 0. A Turing instability requires some q with a positive growth rate, and no such q exists. ∎

The mechanism of the proof is worth naming, because it is structural rather than numerical. A Turing instability needs the two chemicals to be coupled, so that one can act as a local activator and the other as a long-range inhibitor. At the dead state they are not coupled at all: both off-diagonal entries of J carry a factor of v, which is zero. This is the linear shadow of the third structural remark in §1 — where v is absent it stays absent. Chemical v cannot bootstrap itself out of nothing, at any wavelength.

Fig. 4 plots the two branches of (4.1) at the u-skate parameters, and the picture is as unpromising as the algebra: two downward parabolas that start below zero and only fall.

Equation (4.1) also hands over the natural length scales, at no extra cost. Setting the growth rate to zero and solving for an imaginary wavenumber — equivalently, asking how far a small static disturbance reaches before the reaction damps it — gives the two decay lengths

ℓu = √(Du / f),      ℓv = √(Dv / (f + k)) (4.2)

At the u-skate parameters these are ℓu = 1.63 and ℓv = 0.82 grid cells; at the coral parameters, 2.35 and 1.14 cells. Two consequences follow, and both matter later. Structures are only a few cells across, so a grid coarse enough to make them two or three cells wide will misrepresent them. And ℓu > ℓv always, since u diffuses faster and is removed more slowly: the depletion of u around a structure reaches further than the structure itself, which is what lets two structures feel each other before they touch.

Where this leaves us. The dead state is stable everywhere (Proposition 1) and stays stable against every ripple (Proposition 3). So no pattern in this document grew out of noise. Every one of them was put there, at finite amplitude, by an initial condition. The knobs decide what is possible; the seed decides what happens.

5. Bistability, and why the seed decides

With the dead state stable everywhere and a live state that is also stable in the bistable region of Fig. 3, two different final outcomes are available at one parameter address. Which one a run reaches is not determined by f and k. It is determined by which basin of attraction the starting field lies in — the set of initial conditions that flow to a given attractor.

That claim is testable directly, and the test is the sharpest experiment in this document. Fix everything: f = 0.0620, k = 0.06093, the diffusion coefficients, a 128×64 wrapped grid, and 100 000 steps. Change only the starting field.

  • The live seed. Set u = 0.5 and v = 0.3 everywhere, then set v = 0 inside three offset rectangles. This lowers v in an asymmetric shape, on a background that is already alive.
  • The dead seed. Set u = 1 and v = 0 everywhere, then raise v to 1 inside one centred 20×20 square, with a little noise. This is the seeding style the five parameter sweeps in this repository used.

Fig. 5 shows both runs, start and finish.

The outcomes are not subtle. The live seed produces one structure that moves 1.344 px per 1000 steps. The dead seed produces nineteen structures whose centroids move, over a further 5000 steps, by 0.00 px — measured, not asserted.

One detail deserves care, because the simple version of this story is wrong. The dead background does not survive. Its median v rises from 0.0105 to 0.2921 within the first 5000 steps and stays there, which is precisely the live-state value v+ = 0.29209 predicted by (2.3). The live state invades and converts the whole tray. So the seed does not permanently decide the background at this parameter point — it decides the structure that is left standing on it, and that is decided at the moment of nucleation and never revisited.

What separates one travelling structure from nineteen frozen ones is symmetry, and that part is a theorem rather than an observation.

Proposition 4 (a symmetric seed cannot travel). Suppose the initial fields are invariant under a reflection R of the domain that also maps the domain to itself. Then the solution is invariant under R for all time, and the centroid of any quantity computed from (u, v) remains on the fixed set of R. A field symmetric under reflections in two independent directions has a centroid pinned to a point.
The Laplacian commutes with any rigid motion, and the reaction terms in (1.2)–(1.3) act pointwise, so if (u, v) solves the system then so does its reflection (u ∘ R, v ∘ R). Both carry the same initial data, by hypothesis. Solutions of this system with bounded smooth initial data on a compact domain are unique, so the two coincide for all t > 0: the solution is invariant under R. The centroid of an R-invariant non-negative density is itself R-invariant, and the only R-invariant points are those on the reflection axis. With two independent reflections the intersection of the two axes is a single point. ∎

The proposition is checkable, and it was checked. Running the exactly symmetric 20×20 square seed — noise removed, so the symmetry is exact — for 40 000 steps leaves the field symmetric under both reflections to 0.0 in floating point, and puts the centroid of |v − median(v)| at (31.5000, 63.5000) on a 64×128 grid — the exact centre. The three-rectangle seed of the glider is invariant under no reflection at all, which is precisely what buys it a direction.

This is also the explanation of a result already recorded in this repository, at a different parameter address. At f = 0.0682, k = 0.0632 a symmetric single-blob seed settles to one frozen dot, while an asymmetric seed at the same address grows a single extended filament, a worm, whose centroid drifts 6.29 px.

Why 320 tiles found nothing. Five parameter sweeps in this repository searched for the glider by varying f, k and the diffusion rate, across 320 tiles, including two tiles inside the published u-skate band. Every one of them used a dead background with v raised in a compact, near-symmetric shape. By Proposition 4 such a seed cannot produce a travelling structure, and by the experiment above it does not, even when placed exactly on the glider's own parameters and run ten times longer. No refinement of (f, k) could have fixed it, because (f, k) was never what was wrong.

6. The five patterns, placed

Everything needed to sort the five behaviours is now available: whether a live state exists (Theorem 1), whether it is stable (Proposition 2 and Theorem 2), and what the seed does (Proposition 4). The five split cleanly into two families by the first test, then by the second and third.

Read down the "region" column and the unification is visible. Three of the five live in a dead medium and are made of v raised above nothing. One lives in a medium that cannot hold still. Only the glider has a stable live state to be a hole in, and that is what makes it the only one of the five that keeps a fixed shape while moving.

The pair of rows for the soliton and the worm is the strongest evidence in the table, because the medium column is identical for both: same equations, same f, same k, same diffusion, same grid, same step count. Only the seed differs, and the outcomes differ permanently.

7. Scale invariance: one solution is a whole family

Before descending to the grid, one symmetry of (1.2)–(1.3) has to be established, because ignoring it produces a specific and convincing wrong answer — as it did in this repository.

Theorem 3 (diffusive scale invariance). Let (u, v)(x, t) solve (1.2)–(1.3) with coefficients Du, Dv. For any λ > 0, the stretched fields
(ũ, ṹ)(x, t) = (u, v)(x / √λ, t) (7.1)
solve the same system with coefficients λDu and λDv, with f and k unchanged. Multiplying both diffusion coefficients by λ is exactly a spatial stretch by √λ, in the same time.
Write y = x/√λ, so that ũ(x,t) = u(y,t). Each spatial derivative brings out a factor 1/√λ by the chain rule, so a second derivative brings out 1/λ, giving ∇x2ũ = (1/λ)(∇y2u). Therefore
λDu ∇x2ũ = λDu · 1λ ∇y2u = Du∇y2u
The time variable is untouched, so ∂ũ/∂t = ∂u/∂t evaluated at y. The reaction terms uv2, f(1−u) and (f+k)v are algebraic and pointwise: they involve no derivative, so they take the same values at y as before. Substituting all three facts into (1.2) reproduces (1.2) at y, which holds by hypothesis. The identical argument applies to (1.3). ∎

Two consequences follow, and they pull in opposite directions.

The useful one: any structure found at one pair of diffusion coefficients exists at every other pair with the same ratio, simply drawn at a different size. The glider is not a property of Du = 0.164; it is a property of the ratio Dv/Du = 0.5 together with f and k.

The dangerous one: on a fixed grid, raising both coefficients by λ enlarges the structure by √λ in grid cells, so the tray and any fixed-size seed shrink relative to it. A sweep that raises Du at constant grid size is therefore not measuring the effect of diffusion. It is measuring the seed falling out of scale.

Fig. 7 shows the theorem working on two runs already stored in this repository.

The trap, and the number it produced. This repository first walked Du upward on a fixed 128×64 grid and recorded the glider dying at a continuum Du ≈ 0.2735. That number is an artifact of exactly the mechanism above. Repeating the walk while stretching the grid by √λ — which the fractional rectangle seed follows for free — moved the ceiling to between 0.340 and 0.350. The first answer measured the seed leaving the glider's basin, not any limit of the equations.

8. The lattice: where the real ceiling comes from

Everything so far concerned the continuous equations. A solver replaces ∇2 by a weighted sum over a cell and its eight neighbours, and replaces the time derivative by a single explicit Euler step. Both substitutions have consequences that are visible in the measurements.

The stencil used here weights the centre cell by −1, the four edge neighbours by 0.2 and the four corner neighbours by 0.05. The weights sum to zero, so a uniform field is left exactly alone — the discrete counterpart of ∇2(constant) = 0.

Proposition 5 (the stencil's spectrum). On a wrapped grid, the stencil acts on the plane wave exp(i(qxm + qyn)) by multiplication by
L(qx, qy) = −1 + 0.4(cos qx + cos qy) + 0.2 cos qx cos qy (8.1)
and its most negative value is exactly L(π, π) = −1.6.
Applying the stencil multiplies the plane wave by the sum of each weight times the phase factor of its offset. The four edge neighbours at offsets (±1, 0) and (0, ±1) contribute 0.2(e iqx + e−iqx + e iqy + e−iqy) = 0.4(cos qx + cos qy). The four corner neighbours at (±1, ±1) contribute 0.05 · 4 cos qx cos qy, and the centre contributes −1. Summing gives (8.1). Write a = cos qx and b = cos qy, both in [−1, 1]. Then L = −1 + 0.4(a+b) + 0.2ab is affine in a for each fixed b and affine in b for each fixed a. A function affine in each variable separately attains its extremes at the corners of the square, so it suffices to check four points: (1,1) → 0, (1,−1) → −1.2, (−1,1) → −1.2, and (−1,−1) → −1 − 0.8 + 0.2 = −1.6. The minimum is −1.6, attained at qx = qy = π, the checkerboard mode. ∎

Diagonalizing a 256×256 wrapped-grid stencil matrix numerically returns a minimum eigenvalue of −1.6000000000000032, which is the same number to floating-point accuracy.

Fig. 8 collects the stencil, its symbol and the consequence for the time step.

Proposition 6 (explicit Euler stability limit). A diffusion-only explicit Euler step with coefficient D and time step dt is stable if and only if D dt ≤ 2/1.6 = 1.25 in the units in which the stencil above is applied directly.
One step maps the amplitude of the mode (qx, qy) to itself multiplied by 1 + D dt L. The mode neither grows nor oscillates without bound when |1 + D dt L| ≤ 1, that is −2 ≤ D dt L ≤ 0. The right inequality holds automatically since L ≤ 0. The left is tightest at the most negative L, which Proposition 5 gives as −1.6, so the condition is 1.6 D dt ≤ 2. ∎

One conversion completes the picture. The stencil above is exactly 0.3 times the standard Mehrstellen nine-point approximation to ∇2 at unit grid spacing, entry by entry (§B). So a coefficient applied to this stencil is 1/0.3 times the continuum coefficient it represents, and the stability limit in continuum units is 0.3 × 1.25 = 0.375.

An independent check on the whole chain. Proposition 6 plus the 0.3 conversion predicts blow-up once the continuum Du exceeds 0.375. This repository measured non-finite output beginning at continuum Du = 0.370. The glider's own ceiling, 0.340–0.350 from §7, sits just below that — close enough that it is most likely loss of accuracy as the scheme approaches its stability boundary, not a statement about the differential equations.

9. The glider, assembled and checked

Every ingredient is now in place. The glider needs a parameter point in the bistable region of Fig. 3, so that a stable live state exists to be a hole in (§3). It needs a seed that lowers v on that live background rather than raising it on a dead one (§5). And the seed must be asymmetric, or Proposition 4 forbids it from moving. The published recipe satisfies all three.

Four independent checks were run against this document's derivations, and all four agree.

  1. The background is the predicted live state. Integrating a uniform (0.5, 0.3) field with no seed for 200 000 steps settles at u = 0.420870, v = 0.292085. Formula (2.3) predicts u+ = 0.420870, v+ = 0.292085 — agreement to all six decimals quoted. The glider is a hole in exactly the state Theorem 1 constructs.
  2. The speed is reproducible. An independent reimplementation of the recipe, written from the equations rather than copied from the solver, reproduces the stored final field to a maximum absolute difference of 6.0×10−5 in single precision, and moves the structure 6.72 px in 5000 steps — the stored figure of 1.344 px per 1000 steps, to three decimals.
  3. The published band contains the parameter used. The literature reports the u-skate existing for 0.0608833 ≤ k ≤ 0.0609829 at f = 0.06, a window about 1×10−4 wide. The value here, 0.06093, sits inside it.
  4. The stability limit is where predicted. Proposition 6 with the 0.3 conversion gives 0.375 in continuum units; measured divergence begins at 0.370.

The last of those explains why the glider tolerates so narrow a band of diffusion rates. Its ceiling of 0.340–0.350 is not a fact about gliders. It is the explicit Euler scheme running out of room, at 91–93% of its own limit.

10. What this model captures, and what it misses

Everything above is exact for the system in Definition 1. That system is an idealization several times over, and honesty about which parts are physics and which are modelling choices is part of the analysis.

IdealizationWhat it costsWhere it is repaid
Rate constant scaled to 1; concentrations dimensionless no real chemical is identified; f and k are not laboratory rates harmless for the classification here, which uses only ratios
Both coefficients constant and isotropic real gels have structure; real coral grows on a surface it also builds not repaid; the model is an analogy to coral, not a theory of it
Two chemicals, one reaction mitosis here is a shape instability, not biology not repaid; the name is descriptive only
Explicit Euler, dt = 1 accuracy degrades near the limit of §8 the 0.340–0.350 ceiling is measured, and attributed to this
Nine-point stencil on a square lattice the lattice is not rotationally symmetric; travel directions along the axes and the diagonal are not exactly equivalent partly — the Mehrstellen stencil is chosen to reduce this, but not to zero
Wrapped boundaries a structure meets its own wake after one lap the structure re-enters near step 50 900 (Fig. 10); the mass trace of Fig. 11 shows no disturbance
Linear stability only tells what repels, never how far a seed must go to escape a basin not repaid analytically; the basin boundary is found numerically in §5

The last row is the sharpest limitation. Linear analysis around the two stable states, which is what §3 and §4 supply, answers "does a small nudge grow?" and nothing more. It cannot say how large a nudge must be, nor what shape, to cross from one basin to the other. Proposition 4 constrains the answer by symmetry but does not locate the boundary. Everything quantitative about basins in this document is measured, not derived, and that gap is real.

11. Problems

Jump to: Conceptual · Derivational · Computational

Conceptual ↑ contents

C1 — the empty tray. A colleague proposes finding new patterns by starting from the dead state with a very small random perturbation and scanning (f, k) finely over the whole plane. Predict the outcome, and name the result that settles it. Probes: Propositions 1 and 3; §4.
Solution
Every tile relaxes back to the dead state, at every parameter point. Proposition 1 makes the dead state stable everywhere in the plane, and Proposition 3 shows diffusion cannot destabilise it at any wavenumber, because the Jacobian is diagonal there and stays diagonal after subtracting q2D. The scan measures nothing but the decay rates −f and −(f+k). A finite-amplitude seed is required, not a finer grid of parameters.
C2 — two pictures, one address. The soliton and the worm in Fig. 6 have identical f, k, diffusion coefficients, grid and step count. Explain how a deterministic system produces two different permanent outcomes, and say what would have to be true for this to be impossible. Probes: bistability and basins; §5.
Solution
Determinism fixes the map from initial condition to outcome; it does not make that map constant. Both runs converge, but to different attractors, because their initial fields lie in different basins. This would be impossible only if the system had a unique attractor for these parameters — for instance if the live state did not exist (it does not here, this point being outside the fold) and no localised structure could persist on the dead state. Since localised structures do persist, several attractors coexist and the seed selects among them.
C3 — why coral never stops. Coral's live state exists but has positive trace, +2.68×10−4. Mitosis has no live state at all. Both produce endlessly changing pictures. Are the two mechanisms the same? Argue from §2 and §3. Probes: distinguishing "unstable" from "non-existent".
Solution
No. At coral there is a live state to converge towards, but it repels: a region that reaches it drifts away again over roughly 3 734 steps per e-folding, so fronts keep advancing and branching. At mitosis, condition (2.2) fails, so there is no live state anywhere in the phase plane — a spot has nothing uniform to relax onto and resolves its excess by splitting. Both give perpetual change, from opposite causes: one has an unstable target, the other has no target.

Derivational ↑ contents

D1 — the saddle is always a saddle. Prove that the smaller root v− of (2.4) always has det J < 0 whenever the two roots are distinct, so it is a saddle for every (f, k) strictly inside the fold. Probes: Proposition 2 and the root structure of (2.4).
Solution
By Proposition 2, det J = (f+k)(v2−f), so the sign is that of v2−f. The product of the two roots of (2.4) is f(f+k)/(f+k) = f, so v+v− = f exactly. With distinct positive roots, v− < v+, hence v−2 < v+v− = f. So v−2 − f < 0 and the determinant is negative: eigenvalues of opposite sign, a saddle. The same identity gives v+2 > f, so the upper branch always has positive determinant and its stability is decided by the trace alone — which is why the Hopf curve of Theorem 2 is the only remaining boundary.
D2 — a stencil with a different limit. Repeat Propositions 5 and 6 for the five-point stencil with centre −1 and each edge neighbour 1/4 (corners zero). Find its symbol, its most negative value, and the resulting bound on D dt. Compare with 1.25 and explain the direction of the difference. Probes: the method of Proposition 5, transferred.
Solution
Each edge neighbour contributes (1/4)e±iq, so the symbol is L = −1 + 0.5(cos qx + cos qy). It is affine in each cosine, so extremes sit at the corners; the minimum is at qx = qy = π, giving −1 − 1 = −2. The Euler condition |1 + D dt L| ≤ 1 then gives D dt ≤ 2/2 = 1, tighter than 1.25. The nine-point stencil spreads weight onto the diagonals, which softens its response to the checkerboard mode — the corner term +0.2 cos qxcos qy is positive at (π, π) and lifts the minimum from −1.8 to −1.6 — so it tolerates a larger step.
D3 — scaling with only one coefficient. Theorem 3 scales both diffusion coefficients together. Show that scaling only Du is not a symmetry, and identify precisely which step of the proof fails. Probes: reading a proof for its hypotheses; §7.
Solution
The stretch x → x/√λ is a single change of variable applied to both equations at once; it produces the factor 1/λ in both Laplacians. That factor cancels λDu only if the same λ multiplies Dv too. If Dv is left alone, the second equation acquires an uncancelled 1/λ and is no longer of the original form. The invariant quantity is the ratio Dv/Du; changing it is a genuine change of system, which is why the ratio is a real parameter and the overall magnitude is only a choice of length unit.

Computational ↑ contents

K1 — map the fold and the Hopf curve, and place a point. Write code that, for a given f, solves (2.4) for v±, evaluates the trace and determinant from (3.2), and classifies the parameter point into one of the three regions of Fig. 3. Use it to find, for f = 0.0545, the width in k of the sliver where the live state exists but is unstable. Then decide whether coral's own k = 0.0620 lies inside it. Probes: turning §2 and §3 into a classifier; the sensitivity of a near-marginal point.
Solution
The sliver runs from the Hopf boundary to the fold. At f = 0.0545 a bisection on tr = k − v+2 gives the Hopf boundary at k = 0.06198, and √f/2 − f gives the fold at k = 0.06223: a width of 2.5×10−4. Coral's k = 0.0620 lies inside, by 2×10−5 above the Hopf boundary. The instructive part is the margin: a change in k of one part in three thousand moves coral out of the sliver and into the bistable region, which is why its trace is only +2.68×10−4 rather than a robustly positive number.
K2 — find the basin boundary by bisection. Proposition 4 says a symmetric seed cannot travel, but nothing in this document says how deep a dip must be to nucleate a structure at all. At the u-skate parameters, seed a live background with a single rectangle in which v is set to v+(1−ε), and bisect on ε to locate the smallest dip that leaves a structure alive after 20 000 steps. Repeat for two rectangle sizes and report how the threshold moves. Probes: the gap named in §10 — the basin boundary is not accessible to linear analysis.
Solution
A bisection is required because the outcome is a step function of ε: below threshold the dip refills and the field returns to the uniform live state; above it, a structure survives. The threshold depends on the rectangle size as well as the depth, since a dip narrower than ℓv = 0.82 cells is refilled by diffusion from its own edges before the reaction can respond — so shrinking the rectangle raises the required depth, and below roughly one decay length no depth suffices. This two-parameter threshold is the basin boundary, and no result in §3 or §4 predicts it: linear analysis is silent about how far away a basin boundary lies.
K3 — separate the scaling artifact from the real ceiling. Reproduce the two Du walks of §7: one at a fixed 128×64 grid, one stretching the grid by √(Du/0.164) at each step, with the seed specified in grid fractions. Record where the glider dies in each. Then predict, from Proposition 6 and the 0.3 conversion, where the solver must diverge regardless, and check it. Probes: Theorem 3 as an experimental control; a prediction tested against divergence.
Solution
The fixed-grid walk reports death near continuum Du = 0.2735; the stretched walk survives to 0.340–0.350. The difference is entirely Theorem 3: at fixed grid the structure grows as √λ in cells while the tray does not, so the seed leaves the basin. Proposition 6 gives D dt ≤ 1.25 in stencil units, which is 0.375 in continuum units; measured divergence begins at 0.370. The instructive result is that the stretched ceiling (0.340–0.350) sits at 91–93% of the stability limit rather than at some independent value — evidence that what ends the glider is the numerical scheme, not the model.

B. The two diffusion conventions

Two different numbers are called "Du" in this project, and mixing them corrupts every length in §2–§7 by a factor of √0.3 ≈ 0.55. The conversion is exact and worth stating once.

The standard Mehrstellen nine-point approximation to ∇2 at unit spacing has centre −20/6, edges 4/6 and corners 1/6. The stencil used by this solver has centre −1, edges 0.2 and corners 0.05. Entry by entry:

−1−20/6 = 0.24/6 = 0.051/6 = 0.3 (B.1)

So the solver applies 0.3 ∇2 where the continuum equations apply ∇2, and therefore

D(continuum) = 0.3 × D(repo units)(B.2)

Three consequences are used above. This repository's own Du = 1.0 is a continuum 0.30. The published glider's continuum 0.164 is 0.5467 in repo units. And the Euler limit of Proposition 6, 1.25 in repo units, is 0.375 in continuum units — the number checked against measured divergence at 0.370 in §8.
